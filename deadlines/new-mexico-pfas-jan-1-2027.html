Jan 1, 2027

What does New Mexico require on January 1, 2027 for products with intentionally added PFAS?

Beginning January 1, 2027, Section 3 of the Per- and Poly-Fluoroalkyl Substances Protection Act says a manufacturer shall not, and 20.13.2.9 NMAC says a manufacturer may not, sell, offer for sale, or distribute in New Mexico cookware, food packaging, dental floss, juvenile products, or firefighting foam if the product contains an intentionally added PFAS, unless an exemption applies, and 20.13.2.12 NMAC requires a report to the New Mexico Environment Department on or before that date unless an exemption or a currently unavoidable use designation applies, and NMED states the initial report fee is $2,500 and that a reporting waiver request is due no later than December 2, 2026.

A banned product cannot be fixed by reporting or labeling. After January 1, 2027, 20.13.2.13 NMAC says a manufacturer may not manufacture a product containing intentionally added PFAS for sale or distribution unless it is labeled, including on an online listing shown to a New Mexico buyer before purchase, but NMED states that a September 16, 2026 federal injunction currently prohibits NMED from enforcing that labeling requirement while its appeal is pending.

Who it affects

The sales prohibition is written as a duty on a manufacturer. Section 2 of the Act defines a manufacturer as a person, firm, association, partnership, corporation, organization, combination, or joint venture that creates, produces, or assembles a product or whose brand name is affixed to the product. For a product imported into the United States, the importer or first domestic distributor is the manufacturer only when the person who created, produced, or assembled the product, or whose brand name is affixed to it, does not have an office or employees in the United States.

NMED states that the manufacturer responsible for labeling can include private labelers whose brand is associated with the product, and that a wholesaler, retailer, or distributor can accept labeling responsibility. 20.13.2.2 NMAC says the rule applies to manufacturers, distributors, and retailers that sell, offer for sale, or distribute in New Mexico certain products to which PFAS are intentionally added.

The Act defines "intentionally added" as a PFAS deliberately added or used during manufacture where the continued presence, at any level or concentration, is desired or expected in the final product or one of the product's components. Section 3.A of the Act and 20.13.2.10 NMAC list exemptions, including used products offered for sale or resale. They do not state a small-business exemption.

What to do now

  1. Read Section 2 of the Act and decide whether you create, produce, or assemble the product, whether your brand name is affixed to it, or whether you are the importer or first domestic distributor of an imported product whose maker or brand owner has no U.S. office and no U.S. employees. Use the check below. It stays in this browser.
  2. Match the product to the January 1, 2027 list, the January 1, 2028 list, or the January 1, 2032 catch-all. Then read the exemptions in Section 3.A of the Act and in 20.13.2.10 NMAC. Those lists do not state a small-business exemption.
  3. If the product is on the January 1, 2027 list, contains intentionally added PFAS, and no exemption applies, the manufacturer may not sell, offer, or distribute it in New Mexico beginning that date. A banned product cannot be fixed by reporting or labeling.
  4. If a report is required, submit it on or before January 1, 2027 through the New Mexico PFAS Reporting System. 20.13.2.12 NMAC asks for a brief description with a UPC, SKU, or other numeric code; the purpose of the PFAS; the amount of each PFAS by Chemical Abstracts Service registry number, as an exact percentage concentration or in the ranges below, plus documentation of the analytical method; and the manufacturer's name, address, and a contact name, address, and phone number. Initial NMED report fee: $2,500 (NMED). 20.13.2.16 NMAC sets $1,000 for each later report after a significant change. NMED states a reporting waiver request is due no later than December 2, 2026. An extension request must be submitted at least 30 days before the reporting due date. If granted, the rule gives 90 days.
  5. Read 20.13.2.13 NMAC and NMED's September 16, 2026 injunction notice before you treat the label as something NMED is currently enforcing. NMED states that products manufactured before January 1, 2027 may continue to be sold without the label.
  6. If NMED directs a test, 20.13.2.14 NMAC says fluorine above 100 ppm, measured by a commercially available analytical method, creates a rebuttable presumption that PFAS were intentionally added. The manufacturer rebuts that presumption by demonstrating that PFAS were not intentionally added.

20.13.2.12 NMAC lists these reporting ranges: less than 100 ppm (0.01 percent); at least 100 ppm but less than 500 ppm (0.01 to 0.05 percent); at least 500 ppm but less than 1,000 ppm (0.05 to 0.1 percent); at least 1,000 ppm but less than 5,000 ppm (0.1 to 0.5 percent); at least 5,000 ppm but less than 10,000 ppm (0.5 to 1.0 percent); or at least 10,000 ppm (1.0 percent).

That subsection describes polymeric substances whose backbone is either a per- or polyfluorinated carbon-only backbone or a perfluorinated polyether backbone that is a solid at standard temperature and pressure.

FAQ

Which products does New Mexico prohibit on January 1, 2027?

Beginning January 1, 2027, Section 3 of the Per- and Poly-Fluoroalkyl Substances Protection Act says a manufacturer shall not, and 20.13.2.9 NMAC says a manufacturer may not, sell, offer for sale, distribute, or distribute for sale in New Mexico cookware, food packaging, dental floss, juvenile products, or firefighting foam if the product contains an intentionally added PFAS, except as the exemption sections provide. The Act uses the term juvenile products. It defines a juvenile product as a product designed or marketed for use by children under twelve years old, including children's car seats, clothing, and toys, and it leaves listed electronic products out of that definition.

Can reporting or a label fix a banned product?

A banned product cannot be fixed by reporting or labeling. The January 1, 2027 prohibition is a sales and distribution prohibition for those five categories when they contain intentionally added PFAS. Reporting is a separate duty. On or before January 1, 2027, a manufacturer of a product sold or distributed in the state that contains intentionally added PFAS must submit the report unless an exemption or a currently unavoidable use designation applies. On or after January 1, 2028, 20.13.2.9 NMAC also says a product that contains intentionally added PFAS may not be sold unless the required report has been submitted. That reporting rule does not authorize sale of a product the January 1, 2027 prohibition covers.

Who is a manufacturer under the Act?

Section 2 of the Act defines a manufacturer as a person, firm, association, partnership, corporation, organization, combination, or joint venture that creates, produces, or assembles a product or whose brand name is affixed to a product. For a product imported into the United States, the importer or first domestic distributor is the manufacturer only if the person who created, produced, or assembled the product, or whose brand name is affixed to it, does not have an office or employees in the United States. NMED states that labeling responsibility can include private labelers whose brand is associated with the product. The rule's scope also names distributors and retailers. Section 3.A of the Act and 20.13.2.10 NMAC do not state a small-business exemption.

What is the initial report fee, and when is a waiver request due?

Initial NMED report fee: $2,500 (NMED). 20.13.2.16 NMAC sets that same $2,500 fee for the initial report, and a $1,000 fee for each later report after a significant change. NMED states that a waiver request for information that is already publicly available must be submitted at least 30 days before the January 1, 2027 reporting due date, no later than December 2, 2026. The rule also says an extension request must be submitted at least 30 days before the reporting due date, and that a granted extension is 90 days.

Does an online listing need the PFAS label, and is NMED enforcing it?

20.13.2.13 NMAC says that after January 1, 2027, a manufacturer may not manufacture for sale or distribution a product containing intentionally added PFAS unless it is labeled, or documented as labeled consistent with another state's requirements, unless a labeling exemption applies. Where the buyer cannot see the product or package, including online and catalog sales, the manufacturer or retailer must include that label or disclosure before purchase. NMED states that products manufactured before January 1, 2027 may continue to be sold without the label. NMED also states that on September 16, 2026 a federal district court issued an injunction halting NMED's enforcement of the labeling requirement in 20.13.2.13 NMAC, and that the injunction remains in effect while NMED appeals to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit.

What if the product is used, or the only PFAS is a fluoropolymer?

used products: exempt. Section 3.A of the Act and 20.13.2.10 NMAC list used products offered for sale or resale as exempt from the provisions those sections name, and 20.13.2.13 NMAC says the labeling requirements do not apply to used products offered for sale or resale. The rule says used does not include a product returned to a retailer, or offered for resale, without having been installed, operated, or utilized. fluoropolymer: report-exempt, label still applies. NMED states that consumer products containing fluoropolymers are exempt from reporting and product prohibitions under 20.13.2.10 NMAC, and that the labeling requirement still applies. 20.13.2.10.P describes the fluoropolymer products in that exemption.
